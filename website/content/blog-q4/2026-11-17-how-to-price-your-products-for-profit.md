Here's **how to price your products for profit** in one line: add up everything it costs you to sell one unit, decide what share of the selling price you want to keep as profit, then divide your total cost by what's left. If one bag costs you ₦7,200 to sell and you want to keep 35%, your price is ₦7,200 ÷ 0.65, which is about ₦11,100. Round it to a clean price and check it against the market.

That's the formula. The hard part is getting the costs right, because most sellers only count what they paid the supplier. This guide shows you every cost to include, walks through three naira examples step by step, and explains how discounts, delivery and payment fees change your real profit.

## Why "cost plus something" isn't enough

A lot of sellers price like this: "I bought it for ₦6,000, I'll sell for ₦9,000, that's ₦3,000 profit." It feels right. But it ignores the transport to Balogun, the nylon and tape, the data you used to post, the payment fee, and the item that got returned last week.

When you count those, the ₦3,000 "profit" can shrink to ₦1,200 or less. Do that on every sale and you can be busy all month and still wonder where the money went.

:::note Revenue is not profit
Selling ₦500,000 worth of goods in a month means little if your costs were ₦480,000. Profit is what's left after every cost is paid.
:::

## Step 1: Work out your true cost per unit

Your **unit cost** is everything you spend to get one item into a customer's hands, except delivery if the customer pays that separately.

### Direct costs

These are tied to each item:

- **Buying price** from your supplier or the cost of materials
- **Transport to get stock**, divided across the items you bought on that trip
- **Packaging**: nylon, box, tissue, sticker, thank-you card
- **Labour** if you pay someone per item, or value your own time if you make it

### Share of running costs

These are monthly costs you spread across your sales:

- Data and airtime
- Your store plan or app subscriptions
- Ads and content costs
- Rent for a shop or storage space
- Losses from damaged or returned items

To spread them, divide the monthly total by the number of items you expect to sell. If your running costs are ₦60,000 a month and you sell about 100 items, add ₦600 to each item's cost.

:::tip Use last month's numbers
Don't guess how many items you'll sell. Use what you actually sold last month. If you're new, be conservative.
:::

## Step 2: Choose your target profit margin

Your **profit margin** is the share of the selling price you keep as profit. A 35% margin on a ₦10,000 sale means ₦3,500 profit.

There's no single right margin. It depends on what you sell, how fast it sells and how much competition there is. As a rough guide many sellers use:

- **Lower margins** for fast-moving, easy-to-compare items like phone accessories, where buyers check prices across many sellers.
- **Higher margins** for things that are handmade, branded, hard to find or need more service, like custom cakes or curated fashion.

Choose a target, then test it against the market in Step 4.

## Step 3: Use the pricing formula

Here's the formula for how to price your products for profit:

**Selling price = Unit cost ÷ (1 − target margin)**

Write the margin as a decimal: 35% is 0.35, so 1 − 0.35 = 0.65.

### Margin versus markup

People mix these up, and it costs them money.

- **Markup** is profit as a share of *cost*.
- **Margin** is profit as a share of *selling price*.

If an item costs ₦7,200 and you add a 50% markup, you sell at ₦10,800. Your profit is ₦3,600, but your margin is only 33% (₦3,600 ÷ ₦10,800). If you thought "50% markup" meant you keep half the price, you'd be wrong.

Using the margin formula avoids this confusion, because you start from what you want to keep.

## Worked example 1: a fashion seller in Lagos

*Example seller.* Adaeze sells Ankara tote bags she buys in bulk.

| Cost item | Per bag |
|---|---|
| Buying price | ₦6,000 |
| Transport share (₦3,000 trip ÷ 10 bags) | ₦300 |
| Packaging (nylon, tissue, sticker) | ₦400 |
| Share of running costs | ₦500 |
| **Unit cost** | **₦7,200** |

She wants a 35% margin.

₦7,200 ÷ 0.65 = ₦11,077. She rounds up to **₦11,500**.

Now include the payment fee. On Shopinbos, the fee is 1.5% + ₦100, capped at ₦2,000. On ₦11,500 that's ₦173 + ₦100 = about ₦273.

Profit per bag: ₦11,500 − ₦7,200 − ₦273 = **₦4,027**. That's a 35% margin after fees.

## Worked example 2: a food seller in Abuja

*Example seller.* Tunde sells small chops packs for office orders.

| Cost item | Per pack |
|---|---|
| Ingredients | ₦2,800 |
| Pack, foil, bag | ₦350 |
| Gas and power share | ₦250 |
| Helper's pay per pack | ₦600 |
| **Unit cost** | **₦4,000** |

He wants a 40% margin because his packs are made to order.

₦4,000 ÷ 0.60 = ₦6,667. He sets **₦7,000**.

Payment fee on ₦7,000: ₦105 + ₦100 = ₦205.

Profit per pack: ₦7,000 − ₦4,000 − ₦205 = **₦2,795**, about 40%.

:::tip Food sellers: watch waste
If you usually throw away two packs out of every twenty, add that loss to your unit cost. Waste is a real cost.
:::

## Worked example 3: a dropshipping seller

*Example seller.* Zainab dropships kitchen gadgets. She doesn't hold stock. The supplier ships with her shop's label, and she's paid after delivery.

| Cost item | Per order |
|---|---|
| Supplier price | ₦15,000 |
| Share of ads and content | ₦1,500 |
| **Unit cost** | **₦16,500** |

She targets 25%, because similar items are easy to compare.

₦16,500 ÷ 0.75 = ₦22,000.

Fee on ₦22,000: ₦330 + ₦100 = ₦430.

Profit per order: ₦22,000 − ₦16,500 − ₦430 = **₦5,070**, about 23%. That's a little under target, so she either accepts it or nudges the price to ₦22,500. You can browse supplier prices on the [marketplace](/marketplace/) and learn more about [dropshipping on Shopinbos](/features/dropshipping/).

## Step 4: Check your price against the market

The formula tells you what you *need* to charge. The market tells you what people *will* pay. You need both.

:::checklist Market check
Look at 5 to 10 sellers of similar items on Instagram, TikTok and WhatsApp
Compare quality, photos, delivery speed and trust, not just price
Check if your price is far above or below the usual range
Ask a few past customers what they would expect to pay
Decide what makes your offer worth more, if you are pricier
:::

If your formula price is well above the market, you have three choices:

1. **Cut costs.** Buy in bigger quantities, find a better supplier, or use cheaper packaging that still looks good.
2. **Add value.** Better photos, faster delivery, nicer packaging, a bundle. Give buyers a reason to pay more.
3. **Drop the product.** Some items just don't make money for you. That's useful to know.

If your formula price is well below the market, you might be underpricing. Try raising it a little and watch what happens.

## How delivery, fees and discounts affect profit

### Delivery

If the customer pays delivery, keep it out of your unit cost. Show the delivery price at checkout so it's clear. If you offer free delivery, add the average delivery cost into your unit cost, or set a minimum order for free delivery.

### Payment fees

Build fees into the price instead of adding a "card charge". On Shopinbos, the fee is 1.5% + ₦100 per payment, with the ₦100 waived under ₦2,500 and the total capped at ₦2,000. See [payments](/features/payments/) for details.

### Discounts

Discounts cut straight into profit. Using Adaeze's bag:

- Full price ₦11,500, profit about ₦4,027
- 10% off: ₦10,350, fee about ₦255, profit ₦10,350 − ₦7,200 − ₦255 = **₦2,895**

A 10% discount cut her profit per bag by about 28%. To earn the same total profit, she'd need to sell roughly 40% more bags. Discounts can work, but only if they bring in a lot more orders.

:::warn Know your floor price
Work out the lowest price where you still make a small profit. Never discount below it, even in a sale.
:::

## Find your break-even point

**Break-even** is how many units you must sell each month to cover your running costs.

**Break-even units = Monthly running costs ÷ Profit per unit**

If Adaeze's monthly running costs are ₦60,000 and she makes ₦4,027 per bag, she needs to sell about 15 bags just to cover those costs. Every bag after that is real profit.

This number keeps you honest. If you're selling 10 bags a month, you know you need more sales, lower costs, or a better price.

## When to review your prices

Prices aren't set once and forgotten. Review them when:

- **Your supplier's price changes**, which happens often when the naira moves
- **Transport or fuel costs rise**
- **You change packaging**
- **A product sells much faster or slower than expected**
- **Every month**, as a habit

:::steps Monthly price review
List your top products | Start with the 10 items that bring in most of your sales.
Update unit costs | Put in this month's buying price, packaging and running costs.
Recalculate prices | Run the formula with your target margin.
Check the market | Compare with what similar sellers are charging now.
Update your store | Change prices in one place so WhatsApp, Instagram and your store all match.
:::
